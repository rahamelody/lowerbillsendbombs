    This guide isn’t neutral.

    A voter guide with a point of view

    No guide can go deep on every subject, so this one looks at a few clear signals that show what a candidate prioritizes: Americans, corporations, or Israel. If a candidate wants to cut health care, take thousands from real estate and landlord PACs, be against ending the war in Iran, and continue backing Israel…then they don’t serve the needs of Americans today. So we look at:

    • Do they cosponsor the Medicare for All Act, which would guarantee health care to everyone?
    • Do they cosponsor the Housing Is a Human Right Act, which would invest over $300 billion in affordable housing and homelessness services, including $27 billion a year for services, and fund alternatives to criminalizing homelessness?
    • Do they cosponsor the Green New Deal for Public Housing Act, which would fund repairs, retrofits and jobs in public housing and repeal the Faircloth Amendment’s limit on building new public housing?
    • Did they vote to send billions in military aid to Israel, or do they cosponsor the Block the Bombs Act?
    • Do they cosponsor the United States–Israel Defense Partnership Act, which would bring Israel into the U.S. defense industrial base and expand joint military programs? We count that against them, and we do the same for a yes vote on the fiscal 2027 defense bill, which includes a provision expanding U.S.–Israel defense cooperation.
    • Where do they stand on the war on Iran, in their votes and in their own words?
    • Do they take money from AIPAC-linked groups, or from health insurance, pharma, real estate and defense PACs?
    • No voting record? We read what they say on their own campaign website, with the exact quote and a link.

    Why no party labels? We don’t show party affiliations on purpose. A party label is a shortcut. This guide looks at how a candidate has voted, which bills they back, what they’ve said, and who funds them. This is about concrete facts, not party loyalties.

    How bombs are increasing your bills

    The U.S.–Iran war began in late February 2026. Since then the Strait of Hormuz has been largely closed to oil shipments, and the price of the fuel that moves everything has jumped. Here is what the numbers show, and where they come from.

    Gasoline

    $4.12/gal

    up 47% from about $2.80 just before the war. Over the year to September, the CPI gasoline index rose 34.2%.12

    Diesel

    $6.35/gal

    up 67% from $3.81 in late February, after setting an all-time record above $6 in September.13

    Inflation

    3.7%

    over the 12 months to September. Gasoline caused over half of September’s monthly rise, energy prices are up 20.4% on the year, and fuel oil is up 74.2%.2

    30-year mortgage

    7.24%

    versus 6.17% a year ago. Mortgage rates follow Treasury yields, which rose when the war began; S&P Global and Axios both reported the link.45

    From the pump to the price tag

    Diesel moves nearly everything.

    It fuels the trucks, trains and farm equipment that carry food and goods. A freight executive told CNBC diesel “moves through freight rates, farm equipment, food delivery, and home heating.”3

    Shipping costs rise first.

    Producer prices for truck and water freight are up 19.3%, and the Postal Service raised prices 8% in April, citing transportation costs. UPS, FedEx and DHL add fuel surcharges.6

    Farms pay more to grow it.

    USDA raised its 2026 estimates for fuel, lubricant and electricity costs on farms by 33% to 41%, and for major crops’ total costs by 9% to 13%.6

    Then it reaches the shelf.

    Consumers tend to feel it last, “a few weeks after the spike, once surcharges work their way through the supply chain.” USDA now expects 2026 grocery prices to rise 2.4%, up from 1.7%. Airfares are up about 22% on the year, and footwear is up 3.4%.36

    Moody’s Analytics estimates the war is costing the average household nearly $2,000.7

    Where the data comes from

    Money

    • Federal Election Commission bulk files: candidates, committee contributions, independent expenditures
    • OpenFEC API: newest e-filings, refreshed daily

    Votes and bills

    • Voteview: House and Senate roll-call votes
    • GovInfo bill status data: sponsors and cosponsors from Congress.gov records
    • unitedstates/congress-legislators: members, terms, IDs
    • LegiScan: state legislators’ bills and votes

    Candidates and districts

    • Wikipedia: 2026 nominees, race ratings and lead photos
    • U.S. Census Bureau: address and district lookup
    • OpenStreetMap Nominatim and Zippopotam.us: places and ZIP codes
    • unitedstates/images and Open States: official portraits and state legislator photos
    • Candidates’ own campaign websites: quotes, verbatim and linked on each card

    AIPAC-linked PAC money (under Israel / Palestine) = direct AIPAC PAC contributions plus United Democracy Project (AIPAC’s super PAC) spending in support, 2022–2026. Super PAC spending is ads and mailers, not a donation to the campaign. Hover an amount for the breakdown.
